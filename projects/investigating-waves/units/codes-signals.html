Concept 01 · How a wiggle becomes a word

Same message. Different codes.

To send a word across a wire or through the air, you have to turn it into a pattern of pulses. Morse code uses dots and dashes (short and long blips). Computers use binary (0s and 1s). Both carry the same message — just in different secret languages. Watch "WAVES" travel both ways at once. 👇

Live · Encoding PipelineSENDER → WIRE → RECEIVER
The big idea: a letter like "W" isn't really traveling through the wire. A pattern is. The receiver knows the code and turns the pattern back into "W". That's how every message, picture, and video gets across the internet.
Lab 01 · Telegraph key

Tap out your own Morse messages.

Hold down the key. Quick tap = dot. Hold longer = dash. Wait a moment between letters. The telegraph listens, decodes, and spells out your message live. Pause longer for a space. Try tapping your initials! (Press SPACE or click the key.) 🔑

Lab · Live Morse DecoderSPACE bar = tap · or click the key
Fun fact: the SOS distress signal (··· --- ···) was chosen because it's fast and unmistakable, not because it stands for "Save Our Ship." Sailors have been tapping it since 1908!
Lab 02 · Binary translator

Your name in computer language.

Computers don't understand letters — they only understand 0 (off) and 1 (on). Every letter on your keyboard is secretly eight bits of 0s and 1s. Type your name and watch the binary zoom through the wire as pulses of light. 💡

Lab · ASCII Binary Encodertype a word → see the bits
Try this: type your own name. Each letter = 8 bits. The capital "A" is 01000001. That tiny difference is how your phone knows "Cat" from "Dog" — every letter has its own 8-bit ID card.
Lab 03 · Signal vs noise

Can you still read the message?

Out in the real world, signals don't arrive perfectly clean — they get messed up by noise (other waves, weather, static, distance). The more noise, the harder it is to figure out what was sent. Crank the noise dial and watch the message fall apart. 📉

Lab · Transmission Noise SimulatorSENT → CHANNEL+NOISE → RECEIVED
The big idea: engineers spend lifetimes figuring out how to send messages through noise cleanly — repeating them, encoding them with error checks, boosting the signal. That's why your phone works even when the wifi bars are low!
Did you know?

Six wild signal facts.

👽

We're listening for aliens.

The SETI project aims giant radio telescopes at the stars, searching for signals that look like a code — a message from another civilization. No luck yet!

🚢

Submarines talk with HUGE waves.

Normal radio can't reach under water. So submarines use ELF waves with wavelengths thousands of miles long — slow, but they can dive deep into the ocean.

🕊️

Pigeons used to be wifi.

Before radio, armies tied tiny message capsules to homing pigeons — real-life feathered text messages. Some were awarded medals for saving lives!

💡

The internet travels as light.

Under the ocean are cables filled with glass threads thinner than your hair. Your YouTube videos zoom through them as pulses of laser light. 🚀

🎥

A Hollywood star invented wifi.

In WWII, actress Hedy Lamarr co-invented a signal-hopping technique that eventually became the basis of wifi and Bluetooth. Scientist AND movie star!

🛰️

Voyager 1 is still calling home.

Launched in 1977, NASA's Voyager 1 is now 15 BILLION miles away — and it still sends signals back. They take 22 hours to reach Earth, traveling at light speed.
