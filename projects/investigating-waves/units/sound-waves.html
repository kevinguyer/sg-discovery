Sound Waves

Every sound you've ever heard — music, laughter, thunder, your own voice — is the air itself squishing and stretching in waves. Your eardrums are tiny, amazing wave-readers. Let's make them dance. 🎵

SCROLL · TURN IT UP
Concept 01 · Squish-and-stretch

Sound is a pressure wave.

Water waves go up and down. Sound waves go squish and stretch. A speaker pushes on the air, making tiny bunches of compressed air (dense!) and stretched air (thin!) that race outward. When they reach your ear, your eardrum wiggles and you hear. Watch the particles: 👂

Live · Longitudinal (Pressure) Waveair molecules in a row
Watch: each dot only wiggles left-right a tiny bit — but the pattern of bunches travels all the way across. That's why sound can cross a room faster than you can blink (~1,125 ft/s in air!).
Lab 01 · Pitch + volume

Fast waves = high notes. Big waves = loud.

Wiggle the air fast and you get a high-pitched sound (piccolo, whistle, squeak). Wiggle it slow and you get a low-pitched sound (tuba, bass drum, rumble). Wiggle it hard and it's loud — gently and it's soft. You can see the wave AND hear it. 🎚️

Lab · Oscilloscope + Optional Real Toneclick 🔊 to actually hear it
For reference: a dog's bark is about 100 Hz (low). Middle of a piano is 262 Hz. Human scream is about 3,000 Hz. A dog whistle is 25,000 Hz — too high for humans to hear at all! 🐕
Lab 02 · Doppler effect

Why ambulance sirens change pitch.

Have you ever heard a siren go EEEEEEeeoowwww as it drove past? That's the Doppler effect. Sound waves in front of the moving ambulance get squished (higher pitch!). Sound waves behind it get stretched (lower pitch!). Turn up the speed and watch it happen. 🚨

Lab · Moving Sound Sourcetwo listeners: front and back
Watch the spacing: the rings in front of the ambulance are closer together (squished = higher pitch). The rings behind are farther apart (stretched = lower pitch). Same trick works for light waves from stars!
Lab 03 · Echo chamber

When sound bounces off walls.

Yell in a canyon — you hear the echo come back. That's sound reflecting off a cliff, just like light bouncing off a mirror. In a room, every sound bounces off every wall. Your ears hear the original AND all the bounces, mixed into one. Click inside the room to make a sound! 👏

Lab · Room Reflectionclick anywhere inside the room
Try this: click in the corner vs. the middle. Corners make WAY more echoes because the sound bounces off multiple walls. That's why recording studios have fuzzy foam walls — to stop echoes and keep sound clean. 🎙️
Lab 04 · Vinyl records

You can actually see sound, frozen in plastic.

Before MP3s, before Spotify, music lived as tiny wiggly grooves carved into a plastic disc. A needle rides the groove while the record spins — and the same wiggle the singer made in the air becomes the wiggle that pushes the speaker in your room. The entire song is drawn right into the plastic. 🎵

Lab · Vinyl Record & Groove Zoom×1000 magnification
Watch: a HIGH note makes tight little wiggles in the groove — many cycles per inch. A LOW note makes big slow wiggles. A LOUD sound makes the wiggles taller. Every detail of the song is right there in the plastic — and the needle just rides along and retells it! 🪩
Did you know?

Six wild sound facts.

🐬

Dolphins see with sound.

Dolphins make clicks and listen for echoes — it's called echolocation. They can "see" a fish hiding in mud from 100 feet away!

💥

The loudest sound ever.

When Krakatoa volcano erupted in 1883, the boom was heard 3,000 miles away. It was so loud it ruptured eardrums 40 miles away.

🌌

Space is totally silent.

Sound needs air (or water, or something) to travel through. Outer space is empty — so no matter how big the explosion, there's no sound. 😮

🐘

Elephants talk through their feet.

Elephants make super-low rumbles (below your hearing!) that travel through the ground. Other elephants "hear" them with their toes from miles away.

⚡

Sound is slower than light.

That's why you see lightning before you hear thunder. Count the seconds between — every 5 is about 1 mile to the storm!

🦇

Bats scream louder than a rock concert.

A bat's echolocation cry hits 140 decibels — but it's at such a high frequency (ultrasound) that our ears can't hear it at all.
