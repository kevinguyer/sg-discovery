Flip & Fit

Trapezoids, kites, and rhombuses look like they need brand-new rules. They don't. Double them, flip them, or slice along the diagonals — and they turn into shapes you already own.

▼ SCROLL TO FLY
Concept 01 · Meet the odd shapes

Three odd shapes, zero new ideas.

You already have two moves: make a twin and spin it (Unit 02) and cut and slide (Unit 01). Those two moves are enough for every shape on this page.

Trapezoid

Exactly one pair of parallel sides — a long base and a short base. Its twin, spun 180°, makes a parallelogram with base = long + short.

(base₁ + base₂) × height ÷ 2

Rhombus

Four equal sides. Its two diagonals cross at right angles in the middle. Cut along them and you get four right triangles.

diagonal₁ × diagonal₂ ÷ 2

Kite

Two pairs of equal sides that are next to each other. The diagonals still cross at right angles, so the same cut works. Same formula, too!

diagonal₁ × diagonal₂ ÷ 2

Lab 02 · With scissors

The Midline Flip

Cut the trapezoid along the dotted line at half its height. Spin the top piece 180° and tuck it beside the bottom piece. You'll get a parallelogram that is long + short wide but only half as tall. Same area — just one shape now.

Lab 03 · Diagonal cuts

The Kite Cutter

Bonus

Six wild odd-shape facts.

🇺🇸🇬🇧

Trapezoid vs trapezium

In the USA a trapezoid has one pair of parallel sides. In the UK the same shape is called a trapezium — and "trapezoid" means something else. Same shape, different passports.

💎

Diamonds are rhombuses

The "diamond" on playing cards is just a rhombus standing on its corner. Every square is a rhombus too — it's the one whose diagonals are equal.

🪁

Real kites are kites

The classic toy kite is the shape mathematicians named after it: two short sides at the top, two long sides at the bottom, one line of symmetry down the middle.

🥧

The trapezoid table trick

Push two trapezoid tables together, one spun around, and you get a rectangle-ish table for twice the people. Cafeteria geometry!

✂️

The midline is magic

The line halfway up a trapezoid is exactly the average of the two bases: (long + short) ÷ 2. That's the secret hiding inside the formula.

🔷

Half the box, always

Any shape whose diagonals cross at right angles — kite, rhombus, square — has area equal to half its bounding box. Nobody has to memorize a new rule.
