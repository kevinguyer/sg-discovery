Concept 01 · The disguise

A parallelogram is a rectangle in disguise.

A parallelogram is a four-sided shape whose opposite sides are parallel. Rectangles are parallelograms too — they're just the ones with square corners. Take any slanted parallelogram, snip off the slanted triangle on one end, and slide it to the other end. The bumps fit perfectly. You've built a rectangle with the same base, the same height, and the same area.

So the rule is easy

A rectangle's area is base × height. After the slide, the parallelogram is a rectangle with the same base and height. So…

Area of a parallelogram = base × height

Try it yourself below. Cut in different places. Does it always work?

Concept 02 · The trap

Height is not the slanted side.

This is the #1 mistake in the whole unit. The height is the straight-up-and-down distance between the base and the opposite side — it always makes a right angle (a square corner) with the base. The slanted side is longer than the height, so using it gives you an area that's too big. Sometimes the height even lands outside the shape — that's fine, it's still the height.

Bonus

Six wild slicing facts.

🏛️

Euclid did this first

Around 300 BC, the Greek mathematician Euclid proved that parallelograms with the same base and height have the same area — by cutting and sliding, just like you.

🃏

Push a deck of cards

Stack a deck of cards and push the top sideways. It becomes a parallelogram — but it's still the same 52 cards. Same "amount of stuff," different shape.

🔪

Any cut in the zone works

You don't have to cut from the corner. Any straight-down cut between the two dashed lines makes a rectangle after the slide. Outside the zone, the pieces don't fit.

📏

Height can be outside

For a very slanted parallelogram, the height line drops down past the end of the base. Mathematicians just extend the base with a dotted line. Still counts!

🧲

Shearing keeps area

The sliding motion has a name: a shear. Shears change shapes but never change area. Computer graphics use shears to make italic letters!

🎯

Two cuts for extra-slanted

If a parallelogram is pushed over farther than its base is long, one cut isn't enough. Cut it into strips first. You'll try this in Unit 04.
