Chapter 7 · Virginia and the Civil War · VS.7

A House Divided

Harpers Ferry · Richmond · Manassas · Fort Monroe · Appomattox · 1859 – 1865

Two capitals, one hundred miles apart. A state that argued with itself, voted no, then voted yes, and split in two. More battles than any other state, fought on the same rivers and ridges you learned in Chapter 1. And at the center of all of it, the question the founders had left unanswered: could a nation be half free and half enslaved?

◀ UnionConfederacy ▶
Section 1 · The cause of the war · VS.7b

Two ways of living, one question.

By 1860 North and South had grown into different worlds: factories and cities against plantations and fields, free workers against four million enslaved people. People argued about tariffs, railroads, states' rights, and who got to settle the West. Every one of those arguments was really about slavery, and whether it would spread or end.

Section 2 · Abolitionists and the Underground Railroad · VS.7a, VS.7c

The people who refused to wait.

Abolition means officially ending something. Abolitionists wanted slavery ended, and they used every method: speeches, newspapers, lawsuits, secret escape routes, and in one case, a raid on a government armory. Harriet Tubman guided people north through the dark. John Brown tried to start a war at Harpers Ferry. After Nat Turner, Tubman, and Brown, the South decided the North would never leave slavery alone.

Section 3 · Secession and West Virginia · VS.7d

Virginia votes no, then yes, then splits.

When Lincoln won, seven Deep South states left the Union at once. Virginia hesitated for five months. Its convention voted against secession in early April 1861. Then came Fort Sumter and Lincoln's call for troops, and Virginia would not fight its neighbors: on April 17 it voted to leave. The western counties beyond the mountains refused to go along, and in 1863 they became a new state, West Virginia.

Section 4 · Battles in Virginia · VS.7e

Why the war came here.

Washington and Richmond were only about 100 miles apart. For four years each side tried to reach the other's capital, so the fighting stayed in the country between them: the Rappahannock crossings, the railroad junction at Manassas, the swamps east of Richmond, the trenches at Petersburg. More battles were fought in Virginia than in any other state.

Section 5 · Leaders and lives · VS.7f, VS.7g

Generals, spies, soldiers, and three men in a rowboat.

Virginians led both sides. Lee and Jackson commanded for the Confederacy; Winfield Scott, born near Petersburg, stayed with the Union and drew up the plan that won. Two Black Virginians, born enslaved, earned the Medal of Honor. A rich woman in Richmond ran a spy ring, and a formerly enslaved woman spied inside the Confederate president's own house. And in May 1861, three enslaved men rowed to Fort Monroe and started the end of slavery.

Section 6 · Appomattox · VS.7e

Nine days in April.

After nine months in the trenches at Petersburg, the last railroad into Richmond was cut. What followed took nine days: the capital abandoned and burning, Lee's hungry army marching west, and a meeting in a farmhouse parlor at Appomattox Court House. The war that began in Virginia ended there.
