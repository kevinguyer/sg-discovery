Life in the Colony

By 1700 Jamestown's swampy fort had become a colony of 60,000 people strung along the rivers. Almost everything about it, who lived where, who worked for whom, what counted as money, even where the capital sat, came back to one plant: tobacco.

Section 1 · Agriculture · VS.4a

A colony made of tobacco.

Tobacco made Virginia rich and made Virginia what it was. It grew best on fresh river-bottom land, so planters spread up every river and kept moving west when the soil wore out. It needed months of hand labor, so planters wanted workers, more every year. First they used indentured servants. Then, more and more, they bought enslaved Africans. Section 2 · Cultures of the colony · VS.4b

Who lived here, and what they brought.

Colonial Virginia was never just English. It was English planters and their churches, Scots-Irish and German farmers pouring down the Valley with fiddles and big barns, Africans whose skills, music, and foods became Virginia's, and the Indigenous nations whose corn, canoes, and place names the newcomers borrowed. Each group settled in a different part of the land, and each left its mark.

Section 3 · Indentured servants and enslaved people · VS.4c

Two kinds of forced work, not the same.

For its first fifty years the colony ran mostly on indentured servants, people who worked for a set number of years to pay for their passage. After about 1680 it ran more and more on enslaved people, captured in Africa, carried across the Atlantic, and sold. Both worked without pay. Only one system ever ended for the person inside it.

How the trade worked

European ships carried goods to Africa, traded them for people who had been captured, carried those people to the colonies and sold them, then carried tobacco and sugar home. Every side of the triangle made money for someone in Europe. The people in the middle were the price.

Section 4 · Race-based enslavement · VS.4d

How slavery was written into law.

In 1619 there was no law about slavery in Virginia. Some of the first Africans worked a term and went free, like servants. Over the next 86 years, the General Assembly passed law after law that treated Africans differently, until slavery was lifelong, passed from mother to child, and tied to race. These are the laws. Read them as a series of choices Virginia made.

Section 5 · Relocating the capital · VS.4e

Goodbye Jamestown, hello Williamsburg.

In 1699, after 92 years, Virginia gave up on Jamestown and moved its government seven miles inland to a small town called Middle Plantation, renamed Williamsburg for the king. It was a decision, and decisions have reasons.

Section 6 · Exchanging goods and services · VS.4f

Welcome to the country store.

There were almost no coins in colonial Virginia. So how did anyone buy anything? Three ways: pay in tobacco, barter one thing for another, or buy on credit and settle up after the harvest. You have this year's crop, 400 pounds of tobacco, and a deerskin. Go shopping.
