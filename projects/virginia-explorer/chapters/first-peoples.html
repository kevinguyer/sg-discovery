Chapter 2 · Virginia's Indigenous People · VS.2

First Peoples

People have lived on this land for at least 15,000 years. Long before Jamestown, Virginia was home to dozens of nations who spoke three families of languages, fished its rivers, farmed its river bottoms, and built a capital on the York. Their descendants are still here.

Section 1 · Archaeology · VS.2a

Reading the ground.

The first Virginians did not leave written records, so how do we know about them? Archaeologists dig carefully through the soil, layer by layer. Deeper layers are older. Every pot shard, stone blade, or bead they find is evidence, and each new find can change the story. Two Virginia sites changed it a lot.

Section 2 · Language groups · VS.2b, VS.2c

Three families of languages.

Around 1600 there was no single "Virginia Indian" people. There were many nations, each with its own name, leaders, and ways. Their languages belonged to three big families: Algonquian in the Tidewater, Siouan in the Piedmont, and Iroquoian in the far southwest and along the North Carolina line. Where each group lived had everything to do with the land.

    Section 3 · People and environment · VS.2d

    A year in a Powhatan town, around 1600.

    Virginia's early people did not fight the land; they adapted to it. The forest gave wood for houses and canoes. The rivers and the Bay gave fish, oysters, and a way to travel. Deer gave meat and hide. The river-bottom soil grew corn, beans, and squash. What people did depended on the season.

    🌊 Tidewater (Algonquian)Fish, oysters, and crabs from the rivers and Bay · rich soil for corn, beans, and squash · reeds and bark for yehakins (houses) · dugout canoes from big trees
    🌾 Piedmont (Siouan)Deer and turkey in the hills · river-bottom farms along the James and Rappahannock · stone for tools, clay for pots · trade with the coast and the mountains
    ⛰️ Mountains (Iroquoian)Deer, bear, and elk in the forests · valley farms · trails through the gaps for trade · chestnuts, berries, and roots from the forest
    Section 4 · Leading to the present day · VS.2e

    Still here.

    Colonization brought disease, war, lost land, and laws that tried to erase Virginia's Indigenous people from the record. They survived. Today Virginia officially recognizes eleven tribes, and the United States recognizes seven of them as sovereign nations. Several are our neighbors in Central Virginia.
