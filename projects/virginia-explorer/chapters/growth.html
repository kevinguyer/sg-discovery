Chapter 6 · 1783 to the mid-1800s · VS.6d, VS.6e

A Growing Nation

After the Revolution, Virginia's tobacco land was tired and the West was open. Roads pushed through the gaps, canals crept up the rivers, and new machines changed what a farm could do. Hundreds of thousands of Virginians left. Hundreds of thousands more were sold and marched south. And in a Southampton County summer, one enslaved preacher decided he had waited long enough.

Section 1 · Geography and movement · VS.6d

Which way west?

Look at the map and you can see the problem: the Blue Ridge and the Alleghenies stand between the old settled land and the West like a wall. Rivers on this side run to the Atlantic; rivers on that side run to the Ohio and the Mississippi. People went west the way water does, through the gaps, along the valleys, and down the rivers.

Section 2 · Technology · VS.6d

Four machines that moved Virginia.

A new tool changes more than the job it does. The reaper, invented on a Virginia farm, changed how much wheat one family could grow. The cotton gin, invented in Georgia, changed who got sold from Virginia. Steamboats and railroads changed how far anything could travel, and how fast. Virginia's very first railroad ran right through Chesterfield County.

Section 3 · Migration, chosen and forced · VS.6d

Who moved, and who was moved.

People move for different reasons, and not everyone who moves gets to decide. Free Virginians chose to go west for fresh land; that is voluntary migration. Enslaved Virginians were sold to the cotton South and marched away in chains; that is forced migration, and it happened to more than 300,000 people. Both emptied Virginia. Only one was a choice.

Section 4 · Resistance and resilience · VS.6e

Pushing back, every day.

Enslaved people could not vote, own themselves, or leave. But they were not powerless. Most resistance was quiet and daily, the kind that was hard to prove and hard to punish. Open rebellion was rare, because the people who wanted slavery to continue had the militia, the guns, and the law. Both kinds of resistance were about the same thing: keeping some control over your own life.

Section 5 · Nat Turner's Rebellion · VS.6e

Southampton County, August 1831.

Nat Turner was enslaved, could read, and preached to other enslaved people in Southampton County. He believed God had chosen him to strike against slavery. What he and about 70 followers did in two days of August 1831 was the deadliest slave rebellion in American history, and Virginia's response to it shaped the next thirty years. Patrick Henry had asked for liberty or death. Turner's rebellion asked what that meant for people Henry had left out.
