Chapter 5 · Virginia and the American Revolution · VS.5, VS.6a–c

Virginia Declares

Williamsburg · Richmond · Philadelphia · Yorktown · 1765 – 1791

The colony that Britain planted first became the colony that argued loudest, wrote the boldest words, sent the general, and hosted the last battle. When the new nation needed a plan for government and a list of rights, Virginians wrote those too. This is how a colony declared itself a free people, and then helped write what "free" meant.

Section 1 · Why break with Britain? · VS.5a

Eleven years from taxes to independence.

After the French and Indian War, Britain was broke and decided the colonies should pay. Parliament passed taxes that no colonist had voted for. Virginians said their charter of 1606 had promised them the rights of English people, and no English person could be taxed without a say. Britain answered with more taxes, closed ports, and troops. Step by step, argument turned into war. Not every Virginian agreed about it.

Section 2 · The Declaration of Independence · VS.5a

What Jefferson actually said.

In June 1776 Congress asked a 33-year-old Virginian, Thomas Jefferson, to write the announcement. He had George Mason's Virginia Declaration of Rights, adopted two weeks earlier, on his desk. The result is short, and most of it is a list of complaints about the king. But the second paragraph is the part the world remembers.

Section 3 · Contributions, leadership, experiences · VS.5b, VS.6a

The Virginians.

Virginia sent the commander, the author, the pen, the voice, and the planner. It also sent thousands whose names are mostly lost: women who ran the farms, free and enslaved Black Virginians who chose a side for their own reasons, Indigenous people who mostly wanted the fight to go away, and one enslaved man who walked into the British camp as a spy.

Section 4 · Founding documents · VS.6b, VS.6c

Virginia's ideas became America's documents.

Three Virginia documents came first: Mason's Declaration of Rights and the Virginia Constitution in 1776, and Jefferson's Statute for Religious Freedom in 1786. Their ideas were copied, sometimes word for word, into the Declaration of Independence, the U.S. Constitution, and the Bill of Rights.

Section 5 · The capital moves again · VS.5c

Williamsburg to Richmond, 1780.

Virginia's capital has moved twice, and both times the James River had a say. In 1780, with British warships raiding the coast, Williamsburg was too close to the water. Governor Jefferson and the Assembly moved the government to Richmond: 50 miles farther inland, more central as Virginians spread west, and on the James right at the Fall Line.

Section 6 · The victory at Yorktown · VS.5d

The trap at Yorktown.

In 1781 the British general Cornwallis marched his army into Virginia, burned Richmond's warehouses, and then dug in at Yorktown to wait for the Royal Navy. It never came. What came instead was a French fleet, a French army, and George Washington, who had marched 450 miles from New York in secret. Yorktown sits on a peninsula. With the water sealed and the land blocked, there was nowhere to go.
